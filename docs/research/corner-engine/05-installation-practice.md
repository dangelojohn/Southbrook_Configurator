# Installation Practice: How Professional Installers Actually Build Corner Kitchens

Scope: the sequencing and tolerance-absorption knowledge a placement/CAD/MRP engine must respect to generate
buildable corner-kitchen layouts, not just geometrically valid ones. Sourced from manufacturer installation
manuals (Diamond Cabinetry/MasterBrand, KraftMaid/MasterBrand "Momentum", IKEA METOD), NKBA guidance, trade
press (Fine Homebuilding/JLC), and practitioner forums (WoodWeb, ContractorTalk, Houzz, LumberJocks). Folklore
(forum consensus without a citable manual/code source) is marked explicitly as such; everything else is
documented practice traceable to a specific source.

## 1. Layout method: finding the reference plane before any cabinet is touched

Every source agrees installation does not start with a cabinet — it starts with finding the real (non-level,
non-square) condition of the room and building a set of reference lines that absorb that reality.

**High point of the floor.** Floors are never level. The installer runs a straight 2x4 or 4-6 ft level along
the floor at every wall that will carry base cabinets, finds the spot where the floor is highest, and marks
it — that spot becomes the zero reference for every vertical height in the room. Diamond's guide: "Locate the
high point of the finished floor on each continuous wall cabinet run including appliances." The MasterBrand/
KraftMaid "Momentum" guide formalizes this with a toekick offset: measure 21-7/32" out from the wall (where
the toekick line will fall), snap a line on the floor parallel to the wall, then walk a straight 2x4 with a
level on top around the room, marking the wall each place the 2x4's low end touches the floor and the bubble
reads level — the last, highest mark is the high point.

**Level lines struck off that one point (not off the floor everywhere).** From the high point, and *only* from
the high point, the installer strikes level lines around the whole room with a 4-6 ft level or laser — never a
chalk line, which multiple sources call "too fuzzy and not always level" (Fine Homebuilding, 2000). Standard
lines (Diamond, KraftMaid/MasterBrand "Momentum"):
- **34-1/2"** above the high point → top of base cabinets ("Base Cabinet Top Level Line," without countertop).
- **54"** above the high point (= 19-1/2" above the base line) → bottom of wall cabinets.
- **84" / 90" / 96"** above the high point → top of wall cabinets, for 30"/36"/42"-tall wall cabinet lines
  respectively.
These lines are explicitly *not* the leveling reference for any individual cabinet ("these lines aren't meant
to be used to level or square cabinets" — Diamond) — they are the target the tops must land on; each cabinet is
still individually leveled and shimmed up to the line.

**Plumb and square checks, done before cabinets go up.** A 2x4 is held vertically against the wall with a 4 ft
level to find where the wall itself is out of plumb (needs shims at top or bottom of cabinets later). Corners
are checked with a full-size framing square held into the corner: if the square doesn't seat into the corner
it is "in square" (walls converge, corner is tighter than 90°); if the framing square seats but a wall gap
opens away from the corner, it is "out of square" (KraftMaid/MasterBrand guide, "Uneven Walls & Square
Corners"). A commonly cited field check for the corner angle across a run (not found in manufacturer manuals,
practitioner technique) is the 3-4-5 triangle: mark 3 ft out on one wall and 4 ft out on the adjacent wall from
the corner point; the diagonal between those marks should be exactly 5 ft — any deviation quantifies how far
out of square the corner is. Some practitioners repeat the 3-4-5 check at floor, countertop height, and ceiling/
soffit height because a wall can be square at the floor and racked out of square higher up.

**Story poles.** JLC and Fine Homebuilding both document story poles as a coordination tool: a vertical strip
(often a rip of cardboard floor-protection material or plywood) marked with every height on the wall — base
cabinet top, counter height, wall cabinet bottom/top, range hood, undercabinet lighting — transferred from the
single high-point reference rather than re-measured with a tape at every location. A horizontal story pole
version (stapled level across the wall) lets each trade (electrician, plumber, tile setter) plumb its own
layout down from one shared, error-free reference instead of each trade measuring independently and
compounding error.

## 2. Canonical order and why the corner goes first

Across every manufacturer manual reviewed (Diamond, KraftMaid/MasterBrand, IKEA METOD) the rule is the same:
**if the layout has a corner, the corner cabinet is installed first, and both adjoining runs are built outward
from it.** KraftMaid/MasterBrand states it as a decision rule: "If the installation involves a corner, layout
the corner cabinet first. If there is no corner, start at an obstructed end that begins at a wall or
appliance." IKEA's METOD guide: "For a corner kitchen, begin with the corner cabinet... hang the corner base
cabinet first. Each subsequent cabinet is hung in the same way," and stresses "corner kitchens must always be
mounted at a 90° angle" regardless of what the actual wall angle measures.

The reasoning, synthesized across sources:
- The corner is the one place in the room where two runs' geometry is *coupled* — an error here propagates
  into both runs simultaneously, whereas an error at a free run-end only affects that one run and can be hidden
  in an end filler.
- Diamond's guide makes the error-propagation risk explicit for the first (corner) cabinet specifically:
  "1/8" error here will put you 1/2" off 8' down your wall; double check the squareness of your starting
  cabinet" — i.e., the corner cabinet's setting error is a *multiplier*, not a fixed offset, so it gets the
  tightest tolerance and the most scrutiny of any cabinet in the room.
- Susan/blind-corner cabinet hardware (lazy susan trays, blind-corner pullout mechanisms) only functions if the
  corner box itself sits square to both walls; if the corner cabinet is allowed to rack, the mechanism binds.
  ContractorTalk/WoodWeb installer consensus: "all corner cabinets and especially true lazy susans are
  challenging... you must align it to reference lines on both walls as you level, plumb, and attach it."
- Practically, once the corner box is locked square and plumb, each additional cabinet joins face-frame-to-
  face-frame off the previous one, so the corner's squareness is inherited down the whole run — get the corner
  wrong and every downstream cabinet inherits the error.

## 3. The uppers-first vs. bases-first debate

This is a genuine, unresolved disagreement among installers — not folklore vs. fact, but two documented
professional practices with different tradeoffs, both attested in manufacturer literature and trade forums.

**Uppers (wall cabinets) first — the more commonly stated default.**
Diamond's guide states it as the default: "In most kitchens, it is best to begin the installation with the
wall cabinets. This way there won't be any base cabinets to work around and you can more easily use a
step-ladder." KraftMaid/MasterBrand frames it as one of two named procedures ("Installing Wall Cabinets
First"): mark stud lines on the cabinet back, predrill, position with a support/ledger, loosely attach, shim
and level, join face frames, then tighten — "by installing the wall cabinets first, you won't have to reach
over the base cabinets... This will lessen the likelihood of damage to base cabinets." WoodWeb forum installers
add: uppers-first preserves floor workspace, gives better leverage (not reaching over an obstruction), and
pairs naturally with mechanical lifts (Gil-Lift, cabinet jacks) that let one person hang uppers solo.

**Bases first — preferred when appliances or wall cabinets reference off the base run.**
KraftMaid/MasterBrand's alternate named procedure ("Installing Base Cabinets First") is specified for one
concrete case: "If your project layout has wall cabinets that are to be placed directly on the countertop, you
should install the base cabinets first. Once base cabinets are installed, you can place a sheet of plywood or
particleboard on the base cabinets and use them as a work bench" — i.e., the base run becomes the jig surface
for setting wall-cabinet height precisely, using a spacer above the countertop plane. WoodWeb forum installers
give further reasons: the appliance (typically the refrigerator) sets the height of the cabinet above it, which
in turn sets upper cabinet heights, so bases (and appliance openings) must be resolved first; solo installers
often prefer bases first so they can level to the floor and do all scribes and appliance fitting before adding
uppers on top; and in high-end work with tall/full-height cabinets landing on the countertop plane, uppers-
first is "often not an option."

**No universal answer.** Every source that takes a side also qualifies it: the correct order is
project-dependent (appliance heights, whether tall cabinets bridge counter-to-ceiling, whether a lift is
available, granite/countertop templating schedule). A placement engine should treat this as a *configurable
sequencing policy*, not a fixed rule — but the corner-first rule (Section 2) holds regardless of which
policy is chosen.

## 4. Measuring and absorbing out-of-square corners

Once the corner angle is measured (framing square seat test, or the 3-4-5 diagonal check), out-of-square
condition is absorbed by several documented techniques, used alone or in combination:

- **Shim behind the corner cabinet** to bring its back faces parallel to each wall even though the walls
  themselves are not at 90° to each other — the box stays square; the gap it leaves against the wall is filled
  with tapered shims (Diamond, KraftMaid/MasterBrand).
- **Absorb the residual error at the far end of the shorter leg of the L**, not at the corner. KraftMaid/
  MasterBrand: "you will start tight in the corner and shim your cabinets in a straight line... plan on spacing
  out the shortest length of the L." I.e., the corner box is set dead square first; any accumulated
  out-of-square error is walked down the run and dumped into the end filler of whichever leg is shorter,
  because a short leg's filler is less visually prominent and easier to scribe wide without breaking the
  cabinet-to-appliance dimension chain.
- **Undersize/clip the corner cabinet's back corner.** WoodWeb forum installers who build custom corner boxes
  commonly build them intentionally undersized at the back point where the two walls meet, then field-clip or
  belt-sand the clipped corner to match the actual (non-90°) wall angle: reported clip allowances are 1"
  ("I build my corner cabinets undersized by 1" and clip the back corner"), 1/2" (a lighter allowance from
  another contributor), and full-depth reductions in the 8"-10" range for severely out-of-square corners. This
  is practitioner technique for shop-built/custom corners, not manufacturer RTA guidance — treat as documented
  forum practice, not a factory spec.
- **Undersize the depth of cabinets adjoining a lazy susan corner unit.** A specific, quantified technique from
  ContractorTalk/WoodWeb: build the ends of base cabinets that join the susan unit 23-3/4" deep instead of the
  standard 24", so the susan cabinet can be pulled slightly off the wall at the corner to true up its face with
  the adjacent run without the adjoining boxes being physically in the way.
- **Two-piece corner construction with a scribe joint.** Another documented shop technique: build the corner
  base in two pieces, spline the floors together where they meet, screw the top face-frame members together,
  and deliberately leave one leg's butt joint "1/4" shy" so the joint itself can be adjusted/scribed on site
  before final fastening.
- **Filler strips at run ends generally.** Independent of the corner itself, run-end fillers of roughly 1.5"
  are commonly recommended as a standing tolerance buffer against out-of-square walls, so the last cabinet in a
  run isn't the element forced to absorb wall error against a fixed obstruction (appliance, doorway).

## 5. Fastening practice: cabinets to each other before cabinets to the wall

This ordering constraint is stated almost identically across every manufacturer manual reviewed and is one of
the more mechanically important sequencing rules for a placement/MRP engine, because it implies cabinets must
be treated as a *jointly-fastened assembly*, not independently placed and bolted objects.

1. Position, shim, and level each cabinet against the established reference line (34-1/2" base / 54" wall),
   but only **hand-start** or loosely drive the wall attachment screws — do not tighten.
2. Clamp the adjacent cabinet's face frame to the previous one, check both are level/plumb, then predrill and
   screw the face frames together. KraftMaid/MasterBrand's documented predrill spec: 1/8" pilot hole 2-1/2"
   deep through the first face frame and into the second, then a 7/32" clearance hole 1-1/2" deep through the
   first frame only, so the screw's shank slips through the first frame and its threads bite only the second,
   drawing the two frames tight together. Diamond's guide gives the same two-step pilot/clearance method with
   a 9/64"+1/8" bit pairing and specifies **two screws for 30"-tall cabinets, three screws for 36"/42"-tall
   cabinets** at each face-frame joint.
3. **Only after** the whole run's face frames are joined and the run checks level/plumb/straight (checked with
   a 48"-60" level laid horizontally across the run) do the wall attachment screws get fully tightened.
   KraftMaid/MasterBrand states this explicitly as a warning-level instruction: "On base cabinets, don't attach
   cabinets to the wall until all cabinet Face Frames are fastened together." Diamond: "when adjacent cabinet is
   attached to previous cabinet, be sure to fully tighten the previous wall cabinet wall screws" — i.e., final
   wall-screw tightening cascades backward one cabinet at a time as each new joint is made, not all at once at
   the end.

Fastener specifics (Diamond / KraftMaid-MasterBrand, both wood-stud 16" O.C. construction only):
- Cabinet-to-wall: **#10 x 2-1/2"** square-drive or Phillips washer-head screws, into studs on 16" centers.
- Face-frame-to-face-frame: **#8 x 2-1/2"** pan- or trim-head screws.
- Ceiling/soffit or peninsula wall cabinets: **#10 x 3-1/2"** truss-head screws, with the warning that they
  must penetrate at least 1-1/2" into solid ceiling/soffit framing.
- Base lazy-susan cabinets: **#10 x 2"** truss-head screws.
- Never nails or drywall (bugle-head) screws — both manuals treat this as a warranty-voiding error, since a
  flat/bugle head can pull through and drop the cabinet off the wall.
- Minimum stud engagement by cabinet width (KraftMaid/MasterBrand): cabinets ≤18" wide need one wall stud +
  face-frame connection to a neighbor; 21"-39" wide need two studs; 42"-48" wide need three studs (or wall
  anchors/toggle bolts for the third position) and must still tie into at least two studs. An end cabinet where
  a stud falls more than 6" from the exposed end needs a supplemental anchor within 6" of that end.
- A cabinet not bordered by another cabinet on both sides (island end, peninsula) must have its face frame
  additionally supported by the countertop or screwed into ceiling/soffit framing — it cannot rely on wall
  screws alone.

## 6. Corner-specific gotchas

**Blind corner pull / dead space.** A blind corner base cabinet's door and drawer front sit set back from the
open face of the box so they clear the neighboring cabinet's door swing; documented pull distances range from
a 3" practical minimum up to 5"-8" on some hardware/door styles, and the exact figure is commonly finalized
*on site* once the actual adjoining cabinet and its filler are in place, rather than fixed purely from a
catalog spec — because the swing clearance depends on the real filler width chosen for that corner. A filler
strip (commonly 3" for cabinets with drawers, or as little as 1.5" for door-only fronts) is attached to the
blind cabinet's stile specifically to create this clearance; the filler width is therefore functionally part
of the corner mechanism, not decoration.

**Lazy susan / true-corner cabinets need square placement more than any other box in the kitchen**, per
ContractorTalk/WoodWeb installer consensus ("all corner cabinets and especially true lazy susans are
challenging... a two-person installation... 45 minutes to an hour to build and fit up"), because the rotating
tray mechanism binds if the box is racked even slightly out of square — unlike a plain box cabinet, which
tolerates some rack before doors/drawers bind.

**Dead-corner filler returns.** Where a corner cabinet's face doesn't fully resolve the wall-to-wall geometry
(common with blind corners and some susan configurations), a filler panel closes the visual "dead" gap; this
filler's width is one of the values practitioners deliberately leave undetermined until the corner box is
physically set, precisely because it is the tolerance-absorption element for that corner.

**Countertop support at the corner is a separate build step, not automatic.** KraftMaid/MasterBrand: for a
barrel/drum-style lazy susan corner (open top, no cabinet deck), a **1" x 2" wood strip is required at the
34-1/2" line to support the countertop**, and for any void left between a wall/filler and the cabinet run, a
**1" x 3" brace** is installed level with the base cabinet top line, predrilled and screwed into wall studs the
same way as a base cabinet back rail, sized so it leaves **roughly 1" of clearance at each end** between the
brace and the neighboring cabinet — this is a load-bearing detail for the countertop, and if omitted the
countertop has nothing under it at the corner.

## 7. Countertop and backsplash fitting interaction

**Countertops are field-templated after the boxes are set, not built to the design drawing.** Multiple
sources (WoodWeb, Fine Homebuilding, fabricator templating guides) describe the same method: strips of
cardboard or thin plywood are scribed directly to the actual as-built wall and cabinet-run condition once
cabinets are installed and leveled — one strip run along the back of the cabinets scribed to the (possibly
bowed or out-of-square) wall defines the true back edge, a second strip set at the desired front overhang
defines the finished edge, and the assembled template is what actually goes to the fabricator — the drawing is
a starting estimate, the template is the manufacturing input. Standard front overhang is commonly cited at
**1.5"**, with additional allowance needed for any exposed end/peninsula.

**Seam placement rules relative to the corner.** Fabricator and forum sources agree: a seam should never be
placed at or very near an inside corner — cited defect threshold is **within 6" of the corner** — because the
two intersecting runs at a corner already concentrate stress and moisture risk, and a seam there is both
structurally weak and visually the worst spot in the room. The best documented technique for hiding a seam
near a sink run is routing the seam **through the sink cutout itself**, where the sink rim/clips cover the seam
line; if a seam must fall in the sink run without going through the cutout, keep it **at least 10" away from
the sink** and generally on the side opposite the dishwasher. Backsplash tolerance is looser than countertop
tolerance: if a backsplash will cover the wall-to-countertop joint, the scribe fit at the wall can be less
precise, since the backsplash — not the countertop scribe — is the finish surface the eye reads.

## 8. Plumbing and electrical allowances

Rough-in for sink and dishwasher plumbing/electrical happens before cabinets go in, but its geometry
constrains which cabinet can go where and how it must be cut on site — this is general trade practice, not a
single cabinet manufacturer's spec, and figures vary by local code/fixture, so treat these as typical
practitioner values rather than fixed constants:
- Sink drain stub-out: commonly cited around **16" up from the subfloor** to the pipe centerline (a shift down
  from an older ~18" rule, driven by deeper modern sink bowls); hot/cold supply stubs commonly cited in the
  **18"-20"** range, flanking the drain, terminating at an accessible shutoff inside the sink base cabinet.
- Dishwasher: a dedicated 120V GFCI-protected outlet is left inside the adjacent sink base or in the wall
  behind the dishwasher opening, commonly required to be reachable within about **6 ft** of the dishwasher's
  connection point; supply is typically tee'd off the sink hot line with its own shutoff so a dishwasher fault
  doesn't take down the sink.
- Sink base cabinet width: commonly sized at least **3" wider than the sink** to leave room for mounting
  clips, rails, and the trap/supply plumbing inside the box.
- Dishwasher cabinet opening: a common nominal opening is **24" wide x 24" deep x 35" high**, with installers
  advised to leave a little extra rather than cut it tight, since a too-snug opening can prevent the unit from
  sliding fully into place.
All of the above cutouts (utility openings) are explicitly called out in manufacturer guides as a
**pre-installation step** ("before installing any of these cabinets, be sure to measure, mark and cut openings
for plumbing, power sources and other utilities" — Diamond) — i.e., cutout geometry must be resolved before a
box is set, not patched afterward.

## 9. Window and door casing interaction

There is no single universal numeric standard here (unlike the 34-1/2"/54" level lines), but there is
consistent practitioner convergence: leave a visible reveal between the end of an upper cabinet run and the
edge of a window or door casing rather than butting the cabinet directly to the trim. Commonly cited gaps are
**3"-6"** (with 3" repeatedly given as a practical minimum — enough to get a small trim/paint brush behind the
cabinet edge along the wall). This is presented in the sourced material as a design/aesthetic and maintenance
convention (avoids trapping the casing, preserves a sightline/light path, avoids the cabinet reading as
"crashing into" the window) rather than as a structural or code requirement — mark this rule's confidence
accordingly (documented convention, not manufacturer spec).

## Canonical L-kitchen install sequence (step order)

1. **Site survey / demo prep**: remove old kitchen (appliances → countertops/base → wall cabinets), verify
   walls plumb/straight, note window/door/outlet/light locations, rough-in any new electrical/plumbing, repair
   wall/floor surfaces and prime paint before cabinets arrive.
2. **Locate the high point of the floor** across every wall run that will carry base cabinets (straight-edge +
   level walked around the room; lowest floor point cross-checked against the highest wall mark).
3. **Strike level reference lines from the high point only**: 34-1/2" (base cabinet top), 54"/19-1/2" above
   that (wall cabinet bottom), 84"/90"/96" (wall cabinet top, per cabinet height) — adjust downward if a
   soffit/low ceiling constrains the top line.
4. **Locate and mark every wall stud** on 16" centers behind every planned cabinet position; snap plumb lines
   at each stud from floor to above the top wall-cabinet line.
5. **Check the corner and each wall for square/plumb**: framing-square seat test at the corner (and/or 3-4-5
   diagonal check at floor, counter, and soffit height); 2x4-and-level plumb check along each wall for high/low
   spots that will need shimming.
6. **Mark the wall outline / floor outline** for every cabinet in both runs, working out from the corner, so
   the full layout is drawn on the room before any box is lifted.
7. **Dry-fit the corner and first few base cabinets** to visualize the layout, verify drawer/hardware
   clearances at the corner, and measure for any countertop brace needed at a blind-corner void.
8. **Set the corner cabinet first** (base and/or wall, per chosen uppers-first/bases-first policy): shim and
   level it dead square and plumb to *both* walls, hand-start (don't fully tighten) its wall screws.
9. **Build each run outward from the corner, one cabinet at a time**: position next cabinet on the reference
   line → clamp face frame to the previous cabinet → predrill and join face frames (pilot + clearance holes,
   correct screw count for cabinet height) → shim any gap between cabinet back and wall → confirm level/plumb
   → fully tighten the *previous* cabinet's wall screws → loosely secure the new cabinet's wall screws → move
   to the next cabinet.
10. **Absorb accumulated out-of-square error in the end filler of the shorter leg of the L**, not at the
    corner and not mid-run; scribe/rip the filler on site to close the gap.
11. **Install countertop support bracing** at any void (blind-corner brace, barrel-susan support strip) before
    templating.
12. **Field-template the countertop** against the actual as-built cabinet tops and walls (not the design
    drawing); place the seam away from the inside corner (6"+ clear) and away from the sink bowl (10"+ clear if
    not routed through the cutout).
13. **Set countertop, then fit backsplash**, scribing the backsplash to the wall (looser tolerance than the
    countertop-to-wall scribe, since the backsplash is the visible finish surface).
14. **Cut/verify utility openings** (sink, dishwasher, outlets) against the now-fixed cabinet positions before
    final trim.
15. **Re-hang doors/drawers, install hardware, finalize blind-corner pull and filler width on site**, adjust
    hinges, install toe kick, scribe toe kick and end panels, install crown/light-rail moulding.
