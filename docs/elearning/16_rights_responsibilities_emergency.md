# Your Rights, Responsibilities, and Emergency Response

## Who this lesson is for

Every Southbrook employee. This is the last lesson of the WHMIS base
course and it covers two things you need cold: **what you are legally
entitled to** (and obligated to do) under Canadian occupational health
and safety law, and **what to do in the first 60 seconds** when
something goes wrong.

## Recognition + response

### Part 1 — Your three rights under Canadian OHS

Guaranteed to every worker by occupational health and safety
legislation. Not perks Southbrook grants you — legal rights you cannot
sign away.

**Right 1 — The right to know.** You have the right to know about the
hazards in your workplace and how to handle them safely. You are
entitled to **training** on every hazardous product you work with or
near, **access to the SDS** for every product on site, **legible
labels** on every container, and **information about new hazards** as
they are introduced. If your supervisor blocks any of these — that
violates your right to know. Escalate.

**Right 2 — The right to participate.** You can ask questions, raise
hazards, suggest improvements. You can participate in the **Joint
Health and Safety Committee (JHSC)** — Ontario law requires one at
workplaces with 20+ workers. The JHSC inspects the workplace,
investigates incidents, and recommends improvements. You can be a
member; the committee notice board lists current members and how to
nominate yourself.

**Right 3 — The right to refuse unsafe work.** You can refuse work you
believe is dangerous to yourself or another worker. This is the
strongest right and the most misunderstood. The Ontario OHS Act
process:

1. You believe work is unsafe (equipment, environment, or another
   person's behaviour creates a likely danger).
2. **Report the refusal to your supervisor**, verbally is fine.
3. **Stay at a safe place near the workstation** while the
   investigation happens — you are still on the clock; refusal is not
   walking off the job.
4. **Your supervisor investigates** in the presence of a JHSC worker
   representative (or another worker chosen by you).
5. **Hazard resolved** → back to work. **Still unsafe** → the
   **Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development**
   is called; an inspector decides.

You **cannot be punished** for refusing in good faith. Reprisal —
firing, demotion, worse hours, worse station — is a separate offence.

### Part 2 — Internal Responsibility System (IRS)

The three rights live inside the IRS: everyone in the workplace has a
role in safety, scaled to their authority.

- **Workers** — follow safe procedures, use PPE, report hazards.
- **Supervisors** — enforce procedures, train, respond to reports,
  fix what they can.
- **Employer (Southbrook)** — provide safe workplaces, equipment,
  procedures, training, PPE.
- **JHSC** — keep the system honest by inspecting, investigating,
  recommending.

The IRS is layered. Everyone contributes. Everyone is accountable.

### Part 3 — Emergency response

The first 60 seconds determine the outcome. Memorise these.

**Small chemical spill (under ~1 L, non-acutely-toxic):**
Eliminate ignition sources → ventilate → spill kit + PPE per SDS
Sections 6 + 8 → contain with absorbent → scoop into approved waste
container → tell supervisor → log the incident.

**Large spill, or any spill of acutely toxic / corrosive / unknown
product:**
Evacuate the immediate area (move upwind / to fresh air) → alert the
supervisor → sound the building alarm if the spill threatens the area
beyond the room → **do not attempt cleanup** unless trained for that
product with the right PPE on → account for everyone at the muster
point.

**Skin exposure:**
Remove contaminated clothing (cut it off if it sticks) → flush with
water at emergency shower or sink → **15 minutes minimum for
corrosives**, time specified on the SDS for others → tell supervisor →
medical attention per SDS Section 4 → **bring the SDS** to the medical
responder.

**Eye exposure:**
Get to the eye-wash **immediately** — do not stop to ask permission.
Hold eyelids open and flush **15 minutes minimum**. Remove contact
lenses if present and you can do so without delaying the flush. Get
medical attention. Bring the SDS.

**Inhalation:**
Get the person to fresh air. **If you cannot enter without your own
PPE, do not go in — call for help.** Loosen tight clothing. If not
breathing: CPR if trained, call 911. Get medical attention; bring the
SDS. Some inhalation effects are delayed and need observation.

**Ingestion:**
**Do not induce vomiting** unless SDS Section 4 specifically says to
(most solvents — vomiting is the wrong response; aspiration into the
lungs is worse). Call **Poison Control** (Ontario: 1-800-268-9017) or
911. Bring the SDS.

**Fire — wood dust:**
Different from a normal fire. Heat and air movement can flash dust
into an explosion. Sound the alarm → evacuate → call 911. **Do not
use compressed air or a fan** to clear smoke (re-suspends dust). Class
ABC extinguisher for small surface fires. **For a dust-collector
fire: evacuate and wait for the fire department** — opening the
collector can cause a flash.

**Fire — solvent / flammable liquid:**
Sound the alarm → evacuate → call 911. Class B extinguisher if the
fire is smaller than a wastebasket and you have an escape route at
your back. **Never use water** on a solvent fire — spreads burning
liquid. Get out if it is bigger than you can handle in 30 seconds.

**Chain of report.** Every incident — exposure, spill, fire, near miss
— goes up this chain: **immediate supervisor → safety officer / JHSC →
911** for medical / fire / hazardous-materials release beyond a small
spill → **Ministry of Labour** for serious workplace injuries (the
safety officer handles this notification; your job is to make sure the
safety officer knows). Filing a report does not get you in trouble.
Not filing one does.

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"I'd feel like a snitch if I called the JHSC about my supervisor."**
The JHSC exists for exactly this. Concerns are confidential when you
ask. Not raising a hazard because you are uncomfortable is how people
get hurt.

**"I refused to do a task and I'm worried I'll get fired."** Refusing
unsafe work in good faith is protected. Firing or punishing you for
that is **reprisal** — a separate offence. Document everything (date,
time, what you refused, who you told). Talk to the JHSC.

**"I splashed something in my eye but it doesn't hurt much — I'll
finish my task first."** Get to the eye-wash now. Chemical eye damage
is often painless until the damage is done. The 15-minute window is
in regulation for a reason.

**"There was a small lacquer-thinner spill and I cleaned it up with a
mop, then went on with my day."** Mopping is wrong (spreads vapour,
fire risk). And the incident must be logged even if nobody was hurt —
a near miss this week is an injury next week if uncorrected.

**"The eye-wash station was being used as a coat rack and I didn't
say anything."** That is the single most dangerous WHMIS failure on a
shop floor. The eye-wash must be unobstructed and tested weekly. Move
the coats; tell your supervisor.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** Your supervisor asks you to spray a new conversion varnish in
the spray booth. The booth exhaust fan is not working — you can see
the booth fogging up within seconds. What are your options under
Ontario OHS?

> Exercise your **right to refuse unsafe work**. Tell the supervisor
> verbally that you refuse because the exhaust is not functioning.
> Stay nearby — you are still on the clock. The supervisor
> investigates with a JHSC worker representative. Fixed → you resume.
> Still unsafe in your judgement → Ministry of Labour is called. You
> cannot be punished for refusing in good faith.

**2.** A coworker gets contact cement on the back of their hand and
says "it's fine, I'll keep going." What do you tell them?

> Wash it off with soap and water before it cures. Solvent-based
> contact cement is a skin sensitizer; repeated contact builds a
> chronic reaction. Thirty seconds of washing is cheaper than the
> medical leave later.

**3.** You walk past the finishing room and see lacquer thinner
spreading across the floor — maybe 4 L. The supervisor is on break.
What do you do, in order?

> (a) Do not enter the spill area; do not flip electrical switches
> nearby. (b) Yell to clear the immediate area. (c) Call the
> supervisor — phone, radio, or send a coworker. (d) Open doors /
> ventilation upstream. (e) If the spill is growing toward an
> ignition source, **sound the alarm**. (f) Stay out until trained
> responders arrive with the spill kit and respirators. Do not
> attempt cleanup yourself.

**4.** You're a JHSC member. The committee notices three incidents in
two months involving the same edge-banding adhesive. What can the
committee do?

> Recommend the employer investigate and correct — change product,
> change procedure, add PPE, add training. If the employer does not
> respond, the committee can raise it with the Ministry of Labour.
> Right to participate is exercised through exactly this mechanism.

**5.** Someone collapses in the finishing room after spraying for two
hours. They are conscious but disoriented and breathing rapidly. You
are not sure what they sprayed. What is your first call and what do
you bring to the responders?

> Call **911** first. Move them to fresh air only if you can do so
> without exposing yourself (do not enter if the air is still vapour-
> heavy and you have no respirator). Grab the **SDS** for the product
> they were spraying — responders need it for treatment. Tell the
> supervisor. Account for everyone else who was in the room.
