# Safety Data Sheets — Reading the SDS

## Who this lesson is for

Every Southbrook employee. The SDS is the document you open **before**
you use a new product, **when** something goes wrong, and **after** an
exposure to give to the medical responder. It is your primary reference.
It is also the document most workers have never actually read — this
lesson fixes that.

## Where this lives on the site

Southbrook keeps SDSs in two places, both kept in sync:

> **Floor Office → SDS Binder** *(physical binder, alphabetised by product name — exact shelf location: **TBD, trainer to fill in**)*
>
> **Shared drive → Safety → SDS** *(electronic backup, searchable by product name)*

The physical binder is the version you grab in an emergency — it does
not need power or a login. The electronic version is for quick lookup
from your computer when you are planning work.

If an SDS is missing for a product on the shelf, that is a compliance
gap. Tell your supervisor — they will pull it from the supplier and
file it.

## What you'll see in the shop

Every SDS in Canada follows the same structure: **16 sections in a
fixed order**, set by WHMIS 2015 / GHS. The format does not vary by
supplier — Sherwin-Williams, M.L. Campbell, Titebond, and 3M all
publish the same 16 sections in the same order. Once you know the
order, you can find anything in any SDS in under 30 seconds.

The SDS is bilingual — English and French. Both languages must be
provided, either as one bilingual document or as two simultaneous
unilingual documents.

## Recognition + response

### The 16 sections — what is in each

The first nine sections are the ones a shop-floor worker uses. The
last seven (10–16) are technical and regulatory — useful for the
supervisor, the safety officer, and emergency responders.

**Sections 1–9 — work-floor essentials:**

1. **Identification.** Product name, supplier name + address + phone,
   emergency telephone number, recommended use. This is the section
   you read to confirm you have the SDS for the right product.
2. **Hazard identification.** Classification, pictograms, signal word
   ("Danger" / "Warning"), hazard statements, precautionary statements.
   This is the "what's the worst thing this can do to me" summary.
3. **Composition / information on ingredients.** Chemical names, CAS
   numbers, concentration ranges of the hazardous ingredients. Useful
   if you ever have to tell a doctor what is in the product.
4. **First-aid measures.** What to do for inhalation, skin contact,
   eye contact, and ingestion. **Read this before you open the
   container** so you know the response if something happens. Also
   tells you when to call a doctor and what symptoms to watch for.
5. **Fire-fighting measures.** Suitable extinguishing media (water?
   foam? CO2? dry chemical?), unsuitable extinguishing media, special
   fire-fighting hazards. **Critical** if you ever face a fire
   involving the product.
6. **Accidental release measures.** What to do for a small spill vs a
   large spill. Containment. Cleanup. PPE for the cleanup.
7. **Handling and storage.** Safe handling practices. Storage
   conditions — temperature, ventilation, incompatibilities (what
   can't go on the same shelf).
8. **Exposure controls / personal protection.** Exposure limits
   (e.g. 8-hour TWA), engineering controls (spray booth, local
   exhaust), and **the exact PPE you need** — glove material, eye
   protection, respirator cartridge type, body protection.
9. **Physical and chemical properties.** Appearance, odour, flash
   point, viscosity, vapour pressure. Useful for confirming you have
   the right product, and the flash point tells you how easily it
   ignites.

**Sections 10–16 — technical / regulatory:**

10. **Stability and reactivity.** Conditions to avoid, incompatible
    materials, hazardous decomposition products.
11. **Toxicological information.** Routes of exposure, symptoms, acute
    and chronic effects, LD50 data.
12. **Ecological information.** Environmental impact. (Disclosure
    optional in some cases.)
13. **Disposal considerations.** How to dispose of the product and
    contaminated packaging.
14. **Transport information.** UN number, shipping name, transport
    hazard class.
15. **Regulatory information.** Applicable safety, health, and
    environmental regulations.
16. **Other information.** Date of preparation or last revision. **An
    SDS older than 3 years should be flagged** — the supplier is
    required to update it on a 3-year cycle (or sooner if significant
    new info comes to light).

### The five sections you actually use as a worker

If you remember only five, remember these:

- **Section 2 — hazards.** What is the worst this can do.
- **Section 4 — first aid.** What to do if it lands on you, in you, or
  in your eye.
- **Section 6 — accidental release.** What to do if it spills.
- **Section 7 — handling and storage.** How to use it without it
  going wrong.
- **Section 8 — PPE.** What to wear so it does not go wrong in the
  first place.

If you have 60 seconds before starting a new product, read these five.

### How to look up an SDS at Southbrook

1. Find the **product name** on the supplier label (Section 1 of the
   SDS will match it). The product name, not the brand — "Magnamax
   Pre-Cat Lacquer," not "Campbell."
2. Open the **Floor Office SDS Binder** to that letter. Alphabetised
   by product name.
3. Pull the sheet. Read the five core sections (2, 4, 6, 7, 8).
4. Return the sheet to the binder when done — do **not** take it back
   to the shop floor and lose it.

If you cannot find the SDS, do not open the container. Use the
electronic copy on the shared drive instead, or ask the supervisor to
pull it from the supplier.

### When to read an SDS

- **First time** you use a product.
- When a new shipment arrives and the **date of revision** (Section 16)
  is newer than the one in your binder.
- **Annually**, as part of this refresher.
- **Immediately** if there is a spill, an exposure, a fire, or a near
  miss involving the product.
- **Before** any non-routine handling (transferring large quantities,
  mixing two products, disposing).

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"I've used this lacquer for 5 years, I don't need to look at the SDS."**

Suppliers reformulate. A change of resin chemistry can change the
respirator cartridge type, the glove material, or the flash point.
Skim Section 16 of the new shipment's SDS — if the revision date is
newer than your binder copy, replace the binder copy and re-read
Sections 2 and 8 before use.

**"The product spilled, I'll look up the cleanup procedure after I
mop it up."**

No. Read Section 6 first — some products require neutralization, some
must not be mopped (creates more vapour), some require a respirator
for cleanup. Cleaning up wrong can be worse than the spill.

**"The doctor asked what I was exposed to — I just told him 'lacquer
thinner.'"**

That is a start, but the SDS is what the doctor actually needs.
Section 3 (composition) and Section 11 (toxicology) tell the doctor
what to watch for and how to treat. **Bring or send the SDS with the
person to the hospital.**

**"There's no SDS for this product."**

Then the product cannot be used at Southbrook. The employer is
legally required to have one; the supplier is legally required to
provide one. Tell your supervisor — this is a fix-today problem.

**"The SDS is dated 2018 — five years old."**

WHMIS 2015 requires SDSs to be updated **within 3 months** when
significant new hazard information becomes available, and reviewed
regularly. A 5-year-old SDS may not reflect the current formulation
or current regulatory classification. Tell your supervisor to request
the latest revision from the supplier.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** You're about to spray a new conversion varnish you've never used
before. You have 5 minutes before the booth is free. Which sections of
the SDS do you read in that 5 minutes?

> Sections **2** (hazards — pictograms, signal word, hazard statements),
> **8** (PPE — which respirator cartridge, glove material, eye/face
> protection), and **4** (first aid — what to do if you splash it on
> yourself). Section 6 (spill) and Section 7 (handling) are next if
> you have time. Save 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 for your supervisor.

**2.** A coworker has a chemical splash to the eye. Which SDS section
tells you what to do?

> Section **4 — first-aid measures**. It will tell you the eye-wash
> duration (typically 15 minutes minimum), whether to remove contact
> lenses, and when to call a doctor. Get to the eye-wash station first,
> read the section while the eye is being flushed, do not delay the
> flush.

**3.** You spill 4 L of lacquer thinner on the finishing-room floor.
You reach for the mop. What should you have done first?

> Read Section **6 — accidental release measures**. Lacquer thinner is
> flammable; mopping it spreads the vapour and the vapour can ignite
> from any spark. Section 6 will tell you to ventilate, eliminate
> ignition sources, and use absorbent (not water and not a mop) to
> contain it. Many spills also require a respirator for cleanup —
> Section 8 of the same SDS specifies which cartridge.

**4.** The SDS for a product is dated July 2018. Today is June 2026.
What do you do?

> Tell your supervisor and request the latest revision from the
> supplier. Suppliers are required to keep SDSs current. An 8-year-old
> SDS may not reflect the current formulation, current hazard
> classification, or current first-aid practice. Until the new
> revision arrives, the 2018 sheet is the reference you have — use
> it, but do not assume it is up to date.

**5.** Whose responsibility is it to make sure the SDS binder has a
current SDS for every hazardous product on the Southbrook shop floor?

> The **employer** — Southbrook. The supplier is required to provide
> SDSs at point of sale; the employer is required to maintain them
> and keep them accessible to workers. Your job as a worker is to use
> the binder, report missing or outdated sheets to your supervisor,
> and follow the instructions inside.
