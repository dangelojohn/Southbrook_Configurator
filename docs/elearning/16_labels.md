# Labels — Supplier and Workplace

## Who this lesson is for

Every Southbrook employee. You read labels every time you pick up a
container of finish, glue, solvent, or cleaner. You apply labels
whenever you pour a product into a smaller container — for the spray
gun, for a wipe bottle, for a touch-up jar. Getting the label right is
the easiest part of WHMIS to do wrong, and the easiest to fix.

## Where this lives on the site

Workplace-label blanks (pre-printed peel-and-stick sheets) live next to
the relevant decanting stations:

> **Finishing room → solvent decant station → workplace-label dispenser** *(exact location: **TBD, trainer to fill in**)*
>
> **Glue area → adhesive decant station → workplace-label dispenser**

If a dispenser is empty, ask your supervisor for a fresh roll before you
decant.

## What you'll see in the shop

There are **two kinds** of WHMIS labels at Southbrook:

1. **Supplier labels** — printed by the manufacturer or importer, on the
   container the product arrived in. Big, glossy, bilingual.
2. **Workplace labels** — printed or written on at Southbrook, applied
   when we pour the product into a different container. Small, plain,
   may be hand-written.

You also see **transport / hazard placards** on shipping pallets and
delivery trucks. Those are governed by Transport Canada (TDG), not WHMIS,
and are out of scope for this lesson — but if you see one on a delivery
that does not match the supplier label, flag it.

## Recognition + response

### Supplier label — the six required elements

Every WHMIS 2015 supplier label, by law, contains six things. If any one
of them is missing, the label is incomplete and the product should not
be used until corrected.

1. **Product identifier** — the name of the product. Brand, chemical,
   common, or trade name. It must match what is in the SDS Section 1.
   Example: "M.L. Campbell Magnamax Pre-Cat Lacquer."
2. **Initial supplier identifier** — name, address, and telephone of the
   Canadian manufacturer or importer. This is who is legally accountable
   for the product on the Canadian market.
3. **Pictogram(s)** — the red-diamond hazard symbols (lesson 16.2). Zero,
   one, or several depending on the hazard profile.
4. **Signal word** — exactly one of **"Danger"** or **"Warning"**.
   - **"Danger"** is for the more severe hazard categories.
   - **"Warning"** is for the less severe ones.
   - If the product has no signal word, it is not classified as
     hazardous under WHMIS.
5. **Hazard statement(s)** — standardized phrases describing the
   nature of the hazard. Example: *"Highly flammable liquid and
   vapour. May cause respiratory irritation."* These phrases are not
   freeform — they come from a fixed list set by the regulations.
6. **Precautionary statement(s)** — what to do to use the product
   safely. Example: *"Keep away from heat / sparks / open flames /
   hot surfaces. No smoking. Wear protective gloves / eye protection."*

A typical Southbrook supplier label also carries the **product code /
batch number** and **fill date** — useful but not legally required.

### Bilingual rule

Supplier labels in Canada must appear in **both English and French**.
Either on one bilingual label or on two labels (English on one side,
French on the other) attached to the same container. A unilingual
supplier label is **not compliant** — tell your supervisor; Southbrook
will need to apply a bilingual workplace label or get a corrected label
from the supplier.

### Workplace label — what we apply at Southbrook

When you pour a product out of the supplier container into a smaller
container — squirt bottle, jar, cup, secondary tank — that smaller
container needs a **workplace label** unless one of two exceptions
applies (covered below).

A workplace label needs **three** elements:

1. **Product identifier** — the same name that appears on the SDS, so
   anyone reading the workplace label can look up the SDS.
2. **Safe handling precautions** — basic precautions (may include
   pictograms). Does not have to be the full list from the supplier
   label, but should cover the headline hazard.
3. **Reference to the SDS** — wording that points the reader to the
   SDS for full information (e.g. *"See SDS for full handling
   instructions."*).

Workplace labels do **not** need to be bilingual unless your workforce
needs both languages. Southbrook applies bilingual workplace labels by
default — it is good practice and removes ambiguity.

**Two exceptions** where you do not need a workplace label:

- **Decant for immediate use.** You pour the product, you use it in the
  same shift, the container stays under your control the whole time, and
  the leftover is properly disposed of or returned at end of shift. The
  spray-booth pot is the classic case.
- **The original container is undisturbed.** Obvious — supplier label
  still attached, still legible, still intact.

If the product is sitting overnight in a smaller container, even one you
plan to finish tomorrow, it needs a workplace label.

### "Danger" vs "Warning" — a closer look

These are not interchangeable. The supplier picked one based on the
hazard category and you should react to it:

- **Danger** = high-severity category. Lacquer thinner gets "Danger" for
  flammability. An acid catalyst gets "Danger" for corrosion or acute
  toxicity. Treat the precautions as non-negotiable.
- **Warning** = lower-severity category. PVA glue typically gets
  "Warning" for skin/eye irritation. Still real, still requires PPE,
  but the failure mode is irritation rather than acute injury.

### Damaged, missing, or illegible labels

If you pick up a container and the supplier label is torn, faded, washed
off, painted over, or written in a language you cannot read — **stop**.

The procedure is:

1. Do not use the product.
2. Set the container aside in a safe location (not on the spray-booth
   bench, not next to an ignition source).
3. Tell your supervisor.
4. Your supervisor confirms what is in the container — by checking the
   stock record, the SDS binder, or the supplier — and either applies a
   new workplace label or quarantines the container for disposal.

Do not try to identify the contents by smell, colour, or "what we
usually keep here." The whole point of the labelling system is that
nobody guesses.

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"I'm decanting lacquer into a small jar for touch-up — but I'm using
it right now, so I don't need a label."**

Correct only if the jar stays with you and gets disposed of (or poured
back) at the end of your shift. If you leave it on the bench overnight,
the next person to pick it up has no way to know what is in it. Label
it.

**"The supplier label got soaked in lacquer thinner and the words are
gone. I know it's lacquer thinner — I just opened it."**

It does not matter that you know. The next person to walk up to the
container does not. Apply a workplace label or replace the supplier
label.

**"I cut the supplier label off and threw it away because it was peeling
— I'll put a workplace label on later."**

You created an unlabelled hazardous container. The few minutes between
"now" and "later" are when something goes wrong. Label it before you set
it down.

**"This label only has English on it — but I can read English so it's
fine."**

It is not fine. The next worker who picks it up may not read English.
Bilingual is a legal requirement for the supplier label, not a politeness.

**"It's a workplace label, so I'll just write the product name and skip
the safe-handling precautions."**

A workplace label needs three things, not one. If the safe-handling
precautions section is missing, the label does not meet WHMIS
requirements. The pre-printed peel-and-stick blanks have all three
fields — fill them all in.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** You decant 500 mL of lacquer thinner from the 4 L supplier can
into a clean glass jar so you can take it to the assembly bench for
cleanup. You will use roughly half of it today, then put the jar in your
cabinet at the end of shift to use again tomorrow. Does the jar need a
workplace label?

> Yes. Because the jar will sit unattended (in your cabinet, after
> shift), the "immediate use" exception does not apply. Apply a
> workplace label with product name, safe-handling precautions, and a
> reference to the SDS before you leave for the day. Bilingual is
> Southbrook's standard practice.

**2.** A new shipment of contact cement arrives. The supplier label
shows pictograms, a signal word "Danger," hazard statements, and
precautionary statements — but no manufacturer address or phone number.
Is the label compliant?

> No. The **initial supplier identifier** (name, address, telephone of
> the Canadian manufacturer or importer) is one of the six required
> elements. Tell your supervisor; Southbrook needs to follow up with
> the supplier for a corrected label.

**3.** A coworker hands you a bottle with a workplace label that says
only *"Glue."* The bottle is sitting on the assembly bench. What is
missing, and what do you do?

> The workplace label needs the **product name as it appears on the
> SDS**, **safe-handling precautions**, and a **reference to the SDS**.
> "Glue" is not a product name (PVA, contact cement, hot melt, and
> epoxy are all "glue"). Have the coworker re-label with the actual
> product or replace the label before you use it.

**4.** Two pictograms appear on a container — Flame and Health Hazard —
with the signal word **Warning**. Are you safe to skip the respirator
because the signal word is not "Danger"?

> No. The signal word reflects the highest severity category among the
> hazards classified. "Warning" still means real hazard. The Health
> Hazard pictogram drives respirator use — check SDS Section 8 for the
> required cartridge regardless of whether the signal word is "Danger"
> or "Warning."

**5.** You find a 250 mL squeeze bottle in the spray booth with a
faded, illegible label. The liquid inside smells like solvent. What is
the right next step?

> Do not use it. Set it aside in a safe location. Tell your supervisor.
> Identifying by smell is not allowed under WHMIS — your supervisor
> verifies the contents from the stock record or the SDS binder and
> either applies a new workplace label or disposes of the container.
