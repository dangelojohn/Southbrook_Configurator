# The 9 WHMIS Pictograms — What They Mean

## Who this lesson is for

Every person at Southbrook. Pictograms are the fastest way to identify
a hazard — they are designed so you can recognise the risk across the
shop floor without reading the label up close. You should be able to
look at a container at three metres and know whether it is flammable,
toxic, corrosive, or none of the above.

## Where this lives on the site

Pictograms appear on every supplier label and on every SDS (Section 2).
Reference posters are on the wall:

> **Finishing Room → wall by the SDS binder** *(WHMIS pictogram poster)*
>
> **Shop entrance → safety board** *(WHMIS pictogram poster)*

If a poster is missing or damaged, tell your supervisor — Southbrook is
required to keep the visual reference posted.

## What you'll see in the shop

Each pictogram is a **black symbol inside a red diamond on a white
background**, points up and down. A black-and-white diamond is not a
current WHMIS 2015 pictogram — it is either an obsolete WHMIS 1988
symbol (suppliers were required to transition by Dec 2018) or a
non-WHMIS warning. Flag it.

## Recognition + response

### 1. Flame

**Visual.** A flame shape inside the red diamond.
**Means.** Flammable gases, liquids, solids; pyrophoric materials;
self-heating substances; self-reactive compounds.
**At Southbrook.** Lacquer thinner, mineral spirits, nitrocellulose
lacquers, denatured alcohol, acetone, contact cement, most aerosols,
propane cylinders.
**Response.** No open flames, no sparks, no smoking. Use only in
ventilated areas. Bond and ground when transferring. Know the nearest
fire extinguisher (Class B for flammable liquids).

### 2. Flame Over Circle

**Visual.** A flame on top of a circle (the circle = oxygen).
**Means.** Oxidizer. The product itself may not burn, but it feeds a
fire so it burns hotter and faster.
**At Southbrook.** Less common — some bleaches, peroxide wood-bleach
products, warehoused pool/spa chemicals.
**Response.** Keep oxidizers physically away from flammables — not in
the same cabinet or shelf. Fires involving oxidizers are hotter and
harder to put out.

### 3. Gas Cylinder

**Visual.** A pressurized gas cylinder.
**Means.** Gases under pressure — compressed, liquefied, refrigerated,
or dissolved. The hazard is the pressure itself plus whatever the gas
does.
**At Southbrook.** Propane cylinders for the forklift. Some aerosols.
**Response.** Cylinders stored upright, chained, valve caps on when
not in use. Never drop, never expose to heat. Forklift-propane changes
require certification — get trained first.

### 4. Corrosion

**Visual.** Two test tubes pouring liquid onto a hand and a metal bar,
both being eaten away.
**Means.** Destroys metals and burns skin and eyes on contact.
**At Southbrook.** **Acid catalysts** used with conversion varnishes.
Some metal-prep chemicals. Strong cleaners.
**Response.** Chemical-resistant gloves (not leather), face shield,
apron. **Know where the eye-wash station is before you open the
container.** Splash to the eye is a 15-minute flush, immediately.

### 5. Exploding Bomb

**Visual.** A black explosion with chunks flying outward.
**Means.** Explosive. Self-reactive substances (Type A/B) and organic
peroxides (Type A/B) that can detonate or undergo violent runaway
reactions.
**At Southbrook.** Very rare. Some organic-peroxide finish catalysts
carry this in concentration. **If you ever see this on a container,
stop and call your supervisor** — it should not be a routine item.
**Response.** Treat as the most hazardous thing in the room. No heat,
shock, or friction.

### 6. Skull and Crossbones

**Visual.** A human skull with two crossed bones.
**Means.** **Acute** toxicity — a single short exposure (swallowing,
skin contact, or vapour inhalation) can kill or seriously injure.
**At Southbrook.** Rare but real. High-concentration acid catalysts,
some stripping chemicals, methanol if used as a solvent.
**Response.** Full PPE per the SDS — gloves, respirator (correct
cartridge), eye protection, sometimes face shield. Use only in the
spray booth or other properly ventilated area. Wash hands before
eating, drinking, smoking. If exposed: SDS Section 4 (first aid) +
eye-wash or shower immediately.

### 7. Health Hazard

**Visual.** A human torso silhouette with a starburst on the chest,
suggesting internal-organ damage.
**Means.** Longer-term or chronic effects. **Carcinogens** (cancer),
**mutagens** (genetic damage), **reproductive toxins**, **respiratory
sensitizers** (cause asthma after repeated exposure), aspiration
hazards, organ toxicity from repeated exposure.
**At Southbrook.** **Solvent-based contact cement.** Some lacquers
and stains with isocyanates or formaldehyde-releasing resins.
Methylene chloride strippers (legacy stock). Petroleum distillates
in older mineral spirits.
**Response.** Engineering controls first — spray-booth ventilation,
local exhaust. PPE second — respirator with the SDS-specified
cartridge, gloves. The hazard is cumulative — every exposure
matters.

### 8. Exclamation Mark

**Visual.** A single black exclamation mark.
**Means.** Lower-severity hazards. Skin and eye **irritation**, skin
**sensitization** (allergic dermatitis from repeated contact), acute
toxicity that is harmful but not fatal at typical exposures,
respiratory irritation.
**At Southbrook.** Most water-based finishes. PVA / yellow wood glue.
Many cleaning solvents. Some edge-banding hot-melt adhesives. **Wood
dust** is treated under this category in some SDSs.
**Response.** Skin and eye protection, good ventilation. This is the
"won't put you in hospital today, but will mess you up over a career"
warning. Wear gloves even when "it's just glue."

### 9. Biohazardous Infectious Materials

**Visual.** The trefoil biohazard symbol (three interlocking arcs).
**Means.** Biological agents — bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites,
or the toxins they produce. This is the one pictogram Canada kept
from WHMIS 1988 that is **not** in international GHS.
**At Southbrook.** Effectively never. If you see it here, stop and
call your supervisor.
**Response.** Do not handle without specific training.

---

**Note on the "environment" pictogram.** GHS includes an optional
**Environment** pictogram (dead fish + dead tree) for aquatic
hazards. Canada **did not adopt** this for workplace WHMIS — you
will not see it on Canadian WHMIS 2015 supplier labels. It may
appear on SDS Section 12 or transport documents, but not in the red
diamond on the container.

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"The container has two pictograms — which one matters more?"**
Both do. A can of lacquer thinner often carries Flame, Exclamation
Mark, and Health Hazard together. Follow the precautions for all.

**"It's an old can from 2012 and the symbols look different."** That
can carries WHMIS 1988 symbols (circles, not diamonds). Suppliers
were required to transition by Dec 2018. Flag it — outdated symbols
may also mean outdated hazard info.

**"The label is damaged and I can only see one pictogram."** Apply
a workplace label or replace the supplier label. Do not guess at
missing information. See lesson 16.3.

**"I confuse Skull and Crossbones with Health Hazard."** The split
is timing. **Skull and Crossbones** = a single exposure can hurt you
now. **Health Hazard** = repeated exposure damages you over time. A
product can carry both.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** You walk past a 1 L bottle in the finishing room and see two
red diamonds: Flame and Health Hazard. Without reading any words,
what does that tell you?

> The product is flammable (no sparks, ventilate) and causes long-term
> health damage on repeated exposure (use the respirator specified on
> the SDS — never skip PPE because "it's only a little"). Both apply
> at the same time.

**2.** A can of contact cement shows Flame, Exclamation Mark, and
Health Hazard. Skull and Crossbones is **not** present. Does that
mean it is safe to drink?

> No. Skull and Crossbones marks **acute** toxicity — short, severe
> exposures. Many things are not in the acute category and are still
> toxic to swallow. Check SDS Sections 4 (first aid) and 11
> (toxicology). Never drink anything in the shop.

**3.** You see the **Exploding Bomb** pictogram on a small bottle on
the finishing-room shelf. What do you do?

> Stop. Call your supervisor. The Exploding Bomb is rare in a cabinet
> shop and should not be a routine item. Verify with the SDS what is
> in the bottle and whether it should be there at all.

**4.** A coworker tells you "this can has the Corrosion pictogram, but
it's only Category 2, so the gloves we use for staining are fine."
How do you respond?

> Check the SDS, not the assumption. The Corrosion pictogram means the
> product burns skin and damages metals — solvent-rated gloves are not
> necessarily corrosive-rated. SDS Section 8 specifies the correct
> glove material (often nitrile, neoprene, sometimes butyl) and the
> eye-wash must be in reach before you open the container.

**5.** You see a pictogram with the trefoil biohazard symbol on a
container in the warehouse. Is this a normal Southbrook product?

> No. The Biohazardous Infectious Materials pictogram is for
> biological agents. It has no routine use in cabinet manufacturing.
> Stop and call your supervisor — something is mis-shelved or
> mis-shipped.
