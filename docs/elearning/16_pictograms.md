# The 9 WHMIS Pictograms — What They Mean

## Who this lesson is for

Every person at Southbrook. Pictograms are the fastest way to identify a
hazard — they are designed so that you can recognise the risk across the
shop floor without reading the label up close. You should be able to look
at a container at three metres and know whether it is flammable, toxic,
corrosive, or none of the above.

## Where this lives on the site

The pictograms appear on every supplier label and on every SDS (Section 2).
Reference posters are on the wall:

> **Finishing Room → wall by the SDS binder** *(WHMIS pictogram poster, large format)*
>
> **Shop entrance → safety board** *(WHMIS pictogram poster)*

If a poster is missing or damaged, tell your supervisor — Southbrook is
required to keep the visual reference posted.

## What you'll see in the shop

Each pictogram is a **black symbol inside a red diamond on a white
background**. The diamond points up and down, not flat on its base. If
you ever see a black-and-white diamond, that is not a current WHMIS 2015
pictogram — it is either an older WHMIS 1988 symbol (you should not see
these any more, supplier labels were required to be transitioned) or a
non-WHMIS warning. Flag it.

Below: each of the nine pictograms used in Canada, what it looks like,
what it means, and where it shows up at Southbrook.

## Recognition + response

### 1. Flame

**Visual.** A flame shape inside the red diamond.

**Means.** Flammable. The product will ignite from a spark, a hot surface,
or an open flame. Includes flammable gases, flammable liquids (most
solvents), flammable solids, pyrophoric materials (ignite in air), and
self-heating substances.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Lacquer thinner. Mineral spirits.
Most nitrocellulose lacquers. Denatured alcohol. Acetone. Contact cement.
Aerosol cans (most). Propane cylinders for the forklift.

**Your response.** No open flames, no sparks, no smoking. Use only in
ventilated areas. Bond and ground containers when transferring. Know
where the nearest fire extinguisher is — and what class it is (Class B for
flammable liquids).

### 2. Flame Over Circle

**Visual.** A flame sitting on top of a circle (the circle represents the
oxygen molecule).

**Means.** Oxidizer. The product itself may not burn, but it will feed a
fire so it burns hotter and faster. Includes oxidizing gases, liquids,
and solids.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Less common in cabinet work.
Some bleaches, peroxide-based wood-bleach products used by hand-finishers,
certain pool/spa chemicals if stored in the same warehouse area.

**Your response.** Keep oxidizers physically away from flammables —
they are not allowed in the same cabinet or on the same shelf. A fire
involving an oxidizer is hotter and harder to put out.

### 3. Gas Cylinder

**Visual.** A pressurized gas cylinder.

**Means.** Gases under pressure. Compressed, liquefied, refrigerated, or
dissolved gas. The hazard is the pressure itself — a damaged cylinder
becomes a projectile — plus whatever the gas does (asphyxiant, flammable,
toxic).

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Propane cylinders for the
forklift. Compressed-air receivers (these are usually not labelled
because shop air is not a "hazardous product" in the supplier sense, but
the hazard of stored pressure is real). Aerosol products (some).

**Your response.** Cylinders stored upright, chained or strapped, valve
caps on when not in use. Never drop, never expose to heat. Forklift
propane cylinder changes have their own procedure — get certified before
you do one.

### 4. Corrosion

**Visual.** Two test-tube shapes pouring liquid onto a hand and onto a
metal bar; both are visibly being eaten away.

**Means.** Corrosive. The product destroys metals (think rust on
steroids) and burns skin and eyes on contact.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Acid catalysts used with some
**conversion varnishes** (acid-catalyzed lacquer). Some metal-prep
chemicals. Strong cleaners.

**Your response.** Chemical-resistant gloves (not regular leather), face
shield, apron. Know where the **eye-wash station** is before you open the
container. A splash to the eye is a 15-minute flush, immediately. Lesson
16.6 covers emergency response.

### 5. Exploding Bomb

**Visual.** A black explosion shape with chunks flying outward.

**Means.** Explosive. Self-reactive substances (Type A and B) and organic
peroxides (Type A and B) that can detonate or undergo violent runaway
reactions.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Very rare in cabinet work. Some
organic-peroxide catalysts in industrial finishes carry this pictogram in
small concentrations. If you ever see this on a container in our shop,
**stop and call your supervisor** — it should not be a routine item.

**Your response.** Treat it as the most hazardous thing in the room
until proven otherwise. No heat, no shock, no friction.

### 6. Skull and Crossbones

**Visual.** A human skull with two crossed bones behind it.

**Means.** Acute toxicity. The product can kill or seriously injure you
in a single short exposure — by swallowing, by skin contact, or by
breathing the vapour.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Rare but real. **Acid catalysts**
in conversion varnishes can carry this if the concentration is high
enough. Some specialized stripping chemicals. **Methanol** if used as a
solvent.

**Your response.** Full PPE per the SDS — gloves, respirator (correct
cartridge), eye protection, sometimes a face shield. Use only in the
spray booth or another properly ventilated area. Wash hands thoroughly
before eating, drinking, or smoking. If exposed, refer to SDS Section 4
(first aid) and get to the eye-wash or shower immediately.

### 7. Health Hazard

**Visual.** A human torso silhouette with a starburst pattern on the
chest, suggesting damage to internal organs.

**Means.** Longer-term or chronic health effects. Includes **carcinogens**
(cancer), **mutagens** (genetic damage), **reproductive toxins**,
**respiratory sensitizers** (cause asthma after repeated exposure),
**aspiration hazards** (cause chemical pneumonia if vomited), and
**specific target organ toxicity** from repeated exposure.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** **Solvent-based contact cement**.
**Some lacquers and stains** with isocyanates or formaldehyde-releasing
resins. **Methylene chloride** strippers (now rare, but legacy stock
exists). Petroleum distillates in older mineral spirits.

**Your response.** Engineering controls first — spray booth ventilation,
local exhaust. PPE second — respirator with the cartridge specified on
the SDS, gloves. The hazard is cumulative — every exposure matters, not
just the big ones.

### 8. Exclamation Mark

**Visual.** A single black exclamation mark.

**Means.** Lower-severity hazards. Includes **skin and eye irritation**,
**skin sensitization** (allergic dermatitis on repeated contact),
**acute toxicity** that is harmful but not fatal at typical exposures,
and **respiratory tract irritation**.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Most water-based finishes. PVA /
yellow wood glue. Many cleaning solvents. Edge-banding hot-melt
adhesives (some). **Wood dust** is treated under this category in some
SDSs.

**Your response.** Skin and eye protection. Good ventilation. The
exclamation mark is the "this won't put you in hospital today, but it
will mess you up over a career" warning. Use gloves even when the
product is "just glue."

### 9. Biohazardous Infectious Materials

**Visual.** The trefoil biohazard symbol (three interlocking arcs).

**Means.** Biological agents — bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites, or
the toxins they produce — that can cause disease in humans or animals.
This is the one pictogram Canada kept from the older WHMIS 1988 system
that is **not** in the international GHS.

**Where you'll see it at Southbrook.** Effectively never. You should
not encounter this in cabinet manufacturing. It shows up in healthcare,
labs, and waste handling. If you ever see it on a container at our shop,
stop and call your supervisor — there is no routine reason for it.

**Your response.** Do not handle without specific training.

---

**Note on the "environment" pictogram.** The GHS system includes an
optional **Environment** pictogram (a dead fish and a dead tree) for
products hazardous to the aquatic environment. Canada **did not adopt**
this for workplace WHMIS — you will not see it on Canadian supplier
labels under WHMIS 2015. You may see it on an SDS Section 12
(ecological) or on transport documents, but not in the red diamond on
the container.

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"The container has two pictograms — which one matters more?"**

Both matter. A can of lacquer thinner often carries the Flame, Exclamation
Mark, and Health Hazard pictograms together. You follow the precautions
for all of them — not just the scariest-looking one.

**"It's an old can from 2012 and the symbols look different."**

That can carries WHMIS 1988 symbols, which are circles, not diamonds.
Suppliers were required to transition to WHMIS 2015 by December 2018.
A container with the old symbols may also have outdated hazard info —
flag it to your supervisor and check the SDS for the current
classification.

**"The label is damaged and I can only see one pictogram."**

Apply a workplace label or get a fresh one. Do not guess at the missing
information. Lesson 16.3 covers the workplace-label procedure.

**"I confuse Skull and Crossbones with Health Hazard."**

Easy mistake — both involve being damaged. The split is timing:
**Skull and Crossbones = a single exposure can hurt you now.**
**Health Hazard = repeated exposure damages you over time.**
A product can carry both if it is acutely toxic *and* causes long-term
damage.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** You walk past a 1 L bottle on the finishing-room shelf and see
two red diamonds: a Flame and a Health Hazard. What does that tell you
without reading any words?

> The product is flammable (no sparks, no open flame, ventilate) and it
> causes long-term health damage on repeated exposure (use the
> respirator specified on the SDS, do not skip PPE because "it's only a
> little"). Both apply at the same time. You would check the SDS for
> the specific cartridge, gloves, and ventilation.

**2.** A can of contact cement shows a Flame, an Exclamation Mark, and a
Health Hazard. The Skull and Crossbones is **not** present. Does that
mean it is safe to drink?

> No. Skull and Crossbones marks **acute** toxicity — the test exposures
> are short and severe. Many things are not in the acute category and
> are still toxic to swallow. Read the SDS Section 4 (first aid) and
> Section 11 (toxicology). Never drink anything in the shop.

**3.** You see an **Exploding Bomb** pictogram on a small bottle on the
finishing-room shelf. What do you do?

> Stop. Call your supervisor. The Exploding Bomb is rare in a cabinet
> shop and should not be a routine item — it indicates a substance that
> can detonate or undergo a violent runaway reaction. Verify with the
> SDS what is in the bottle and whether it should be there at all.

**4.** A coworker tells you "this can has the Corrosion pictogram, but
it's only Category 2, so the gloves we use for staining are fine." How
do you respond?

> Check the SDS, not the assumption. The Corrosion pictogram means the
> product burns skin and damages metals — gloves rated for solvents are
> not necessarily rated for corrosives. SDS Section 8 specifies the
> correct glove material (often nitrile or neoprene, sometimes butyl)
> and the eye-wash station must be in reach before you open the
> container.

**5.** You see a pictogram with the trefoil biohazard symbol on a
container in the warehouse. Is this a normal Southbrook product?

> No. The Biohazardous Infectious Materials pictogram is for biological
> agents (bacteria, viruses, fungi). It has no routine use in cabinet
> manufacturing. If you see it on a Southbrook container, stop and call
> your supervisor — something is mis-shelved or mis-shipped.
