# WHMIS Overview — What It Is and Why It Matters

## Who this lesson is for

You. Every person who works at Southbrook Cabinetry — shop floor, finishing
room, warehouse, office, delivery driver, summer student — takes this
lesson on hire and once a year after that. It does not matter whether you
ever pour a litre of lacquer thinner; if you set foot in the shop, you are
covered by WHMIS and you need to know what it is.

This is a **base awareness** lesson. It does **not** make you certified to
run the spray booth, to operate the forklift, or to handle the acid
catalysts used with some conversion varnishes. Those tasks have their own
hands-on training and sign-off. WHMIS is the foundation everyone shares.

## What you'll see in the shop

WHMIS shows up in three places on the floor. Once you know what they look
like, you stop missing them.

- **Labels** on every container of a hazardous product. A 4 L can of
  lacquer thinner from the supplier has a printed label with red-diamond
  pictograms, a signal word ("Danger" or "Warning"), and a list of
  precautions. When you decant some of that thinner into a smaller squirt
  bottle for cleanup, that bottle gets a **workplace label** — see
  lesson 16.3.
- **Safety Data Sheets** (SDS) — one paper sheet per product, filed in
  the SDS binder. Each is a 16-section document the supplier writes.
  Lesson 16.4 walks you through it.
- **Training** — this course, plus job-specific training when your role
  involves handling a particular product (spray booth, finishing, glue
  line). Training is the third pillar; without it, the labels and SDSs
  are just paper.

## Recognition + response

**What WHMIS is.** WHMIS stands for **Workplace Hazardous Materials
Information System**. It is the Canada-wide system that tells you what
hazardous chemicals are in your workplace and how to use them safely. It
has been law since 1988. The current version — **WHMIS 2015** — aligns
Canada with the **Globally Harmonized System (GHS)**, which is the
international standard the United Nations adopted so a chemical labelled
in Germany or Japan looks the same to a Canadian worker reading the
container.

**The three pillars.** WHMIS works because three things happen together:

1. **Labels** on every container.
2. **Safety Data Sheets** (SDSs) available to every worker.
3. **Education and training** so workers can read both.

Remove any one of the three and the system fails. A perfect label on a
sealed drum is useless if you cannot read it. A binder full of SDSs is
useless if no one ever opens it.

**The legal frame.** WHMIS is split across federal and provincial law:

- **Federal** — the **Hazardous Products Act** and the **Hazardous
  Products Regulations** set the rules suppliers and importers must
  follow when they sell a hazardous product into Canada. That is why the
  label on a can of lacquer thinner shipped from Ohio looks the way it
  does — the importer had to bring it into compliance.
- **Provincial / territorial** — each province's **Occupational Health
  and Safety Act** sets the rules **employers and workers** must follow
  once the product is in the workplace. Southbrook operates in Ontario,
  so Ontario's OHS Act and Reg. 860 (WHMIS) govern what we do here.

If you ever move to a shop in another province, the federal piece is
identical and the provincial piece will look very similar — the three
pillars do not change.

**Employer responsibilities.** Southbrook (your employer) must:

- Make sure every hazardous product in the shop has a current supplier
  label or a workplace label.
- Keep a current SDS for every hazardous product, accessible to workers.
- Provide WHMIS education (this course) and job-specific training.
- Review the training at least annually and whenever a new hazard is
  introduced.

**Your responsibilities.** As an employee you must:

- Complete WHMIS training and the annual refresher.
- Read labels and follow the precautions before you use a product.
- Use the personal protective equipment (PPE) specified for the job.
- Report damaged labels, missing SDSs, leaks, spills, or unsafe
  conditions to your supervisor — without waiting.
- Ask if you do not know. There is never a wrong time to ask about a
  chemical.

**Bilingual rule.** In Canada, supplier labels and SDSs must be available
in **both English and French**. If you find a container with only one
language, that is a compliance gap — tell your supervisor.

## Common mistakes + how to recover

**"It's just a small amount, I don't need to read the label."**

The amount in the container has nothing to do with the hazard of the
product. A 250 mL squirt bottle of lacquer thinner is just as flammable
as a 4 L can. Read the label.

**"I've used this product for years, I know what it does."**

Formulations change. The chemistry of a finish product can be updated by
the supplier between batches. The label and the SDS are the source of
truth, not your memory. Skim them when a new batch shows up.

**"There's no SDS in the binder for this product."**

Stop using the product. Tell your supervisor. Southbrook is legally
required to have one and the supplier is legally required to provide it.
This is not a small problem — it is the system failing.

**"My English is weak and the label is hard for me to read."**

The label has to be in English **and** French. Pictograms are designed to
be understood without words. Ask your supervisor for the French label or
for someone to walk through it with you. WHMIS is built so that no worker
ever has to guess.

## Quiz (5 questions, applied)

**1.** A coworker hands you a clear plastic squeeze bottle of liquid and
says "use this to wipe down the panels before staining." There is no
label on the bottle. What do you do?

> Do not use it. An unlabelled container is a WHMIS violation. Ask the
> coworker what is in it and have them apply a proper workplace label
> (product name, safe-handling precautions, reference to the SDS) before
> you go near it. If they cannot tell you what is in it, treat it as
> unknown and tell your supervisor.

**2.** Which of the following is **not** one of the three pillars of
WHMIS? (a) Labels, (b) Safety Data Sheets, (c) Education and training,
(d) First-aid kits.

> (d) First-aid kits. First aid is required under provincial OHS law but
> it is not a WHMIS pillar. The three WHMIS pillars are labels, SDSs,
> and education/training.

**3.** A new can of contact cement arrives from the supplier. The label
is printed only in English — there is no French. Is this a problem?

> Yes. WHMIS 2015 requires supplier labels to be in both English and
> French. Tell your supervisor; Southbrook may need to apply a
> bilingual workplace label or contact the supplier for a corrected
> label.

**4.** Whose job is it to make sure the SDS binder is up to date?

> The employer's — Southbrook's. The supplier provides the SDSs at
> point of sale; Southbrook maintains the binder and keeps it
> accessible to workers. Your job is to use it and to report when an
> SDS is missing.

**5.** A summer student starts on Monday and is sent to help in the
finishing room. They have not taken WHMIS training yet. What does
Southbrook owe them before they handle any product?

> WHMIS education before they work with or near hazardous products.
> Ontario OHS does not allow "we'll train you later." The student
> takes this base lesson first, then the job-specific finishing-room
> training, then they can go near the product.
